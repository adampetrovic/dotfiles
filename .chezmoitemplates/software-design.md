## Software design rubric

Use this synthesis of 14 supplied design, architecture, testing, security, and reliability books when planning, implementing, or reviewing code. Apply relevant principles with judgment, within the brief, repository conventions, role permissions, and existing output format. A heuristic or named pattern is not proof of a defect or permission for broader work.

1. **Frame the change.** Identify the intended outcome, current behaviour, constraints, and assumptions that could change the solution. Distinguish a behaviour change from behaviour-preserving refactoring and performance work. Scale investigation to risk; a small local fix does not need an architecture exercise.
2. **Compare consequential options.** For a material design decision, compare at least two plausible approaches at similar scope, including keeping the current design when viable. Weigh caller effort, ownership, complexity, compatibility, failure behaviour, and relevant quality requirements. Use a bounded experiment only for unresolved uncertainty; distinguish disposable prototypes from production work.
3. **Reduce total complexity.** Look for change amplification, cognitive load, and hidden update obligations. Prefer substantial capability behind a small, complete interface. Count call ordering, errors, configuration, and cleanup as interface costs. Pull related complexity into its owner; do not hide facts callers need for correctness. Extra layers, tiny methods, wrappers, and generality must earn their cost.
4. **Own knowledge and change together.** Encapsulate representations and rules that must stay consistent. Assess coupling by shared knowledge, distance, and volatility, not dependency count alone. Similar text need not express one rule; extracting it can couple independent policies. Keep essential invariant relationships close, separate unrelated policy, and avoid speculative reuse or distribution.
5. **Make contracts explicit.** Use precise names and suitable types for domain meaning, units, absence, ownership, and lifecycle. Prevent invalid states where practical and enforce invariants at construction and mutation boundaries. Draft important interface contracts before bodies; document nonobvious semantics and rationale, not statements already obvious from code. Follow local conventions and update comments with code.
6. **Control state and effects.** Separate deterministic decisions from I/O, clocks, and shared state when that simplifies reasoning and tests. Make implicit inputs, aliases, side effects, and resource cleanup visible. Immutability is not automatically deep or concurrency-safe. Identify atomicity and ordering requirements; verify the scope of locks, queues, snapshots, and transactions rather than assuming they coordinate remote effects.
7. **Preserve meaningful failures.** Design harmless cases into normal semantics only when the contract permits. Recover where reliable, aggregate common recovery at a responsible boundary, and preserve diagnostic causes. Never turn unknown failure into empty success or silently discard required work. Distinguish request rejection, overload refusal, and unrecoverable failure; fail-fast does not mean crash every service.
8. **Change safely.** Protect threatened observable behaviour with focused tests. Characterisation captures what legacy code does, not what it should do. When no harness is reachable, make the smallest inspected test-enabling seam before broader changes. Use small transformations and compatible migrations; keep intended behaviour changes distinct from restructuring. Report unverified callers, environments, or contracts.
9. **Test useful guarantees.** Test outcomes, invariants, boundaries, failures, and supported effects. Prefer tests that survive harmless refactoring; do not freeze private helpers or incidental call sequences. Doubles do not prove real dependency behaviour. Use integration checks where needed and property-based tests where useful. Neither coverage nor passing examples proves correctness; follow the project's testing workflow without prescribing one universal TDD or mocking school.
10. **Respect trust and capacity.** Validate untrusted data before expensive work; preserve authorisation, parameterised queries, and context-specific encoding. Keep secrets and sensitive payloads out of errors and logs. Bound relevant waits, queues, input expansion, and resource accumulation. Before replaying a side effect, establish idempotency or a safe recovery contract: a timeout does not prove remote failure.
11. **Measure and operate deliberately.** Prefer naturally efficient designs; require representative measurements or established requirements before accepting optimisation complexity. Verify improvement against a baseline. For production-facing changes, consider partial failure, overload, observability, mixed-version compatibility, migration, and recovery. Do not run load, chaos, deployment, or production experiments without permission.
12. **Keep recommendations proportionate.** Improve the affected design incrementally. Explain material trade-offs and escalate broader redesign needs rather than silently widening scope. A review finding needs a concrete consequence and proportionate remedy. No findings is valid; do not demand a pattern, a framework, or a design essay without a demonstrated need.

It contains source anchors, competing recommendations, and conditional guidance for legacy changes, architecture, domain models, concurrency, security, and production failures.
